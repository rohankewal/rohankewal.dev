Squarespace is a good first website. It is a bad fifth year.

That isn't a criticism of the platform. Squarespace does exactly what it set out to do: get a business online quickly, looking respectable, without a developer. For a lot of companies that is the correct decision for the first two or three years, and I'd tell a new client the same thing if their needs were genuinely that simple.

The problem is that businesses grow and platforms don't grow with them. The ceiling arrives quietly. You don't wake up one morning locked out — you just find yourself spending more and more time working around your own website.

Here are the five signs I see most often, and what each one actually means underneath.

## 1. Your blog won't rank

You've published twenty posts. None of them show up in search for anything you'd care about.

The instinct is to blame the writing. Usually the writing is fine. What's missing is the structural control that search engines reward: clean heading hierarchy you actually decide, schema markup that describes what the page is, internal linking you can shape deliberately, canonical tags, control over how the URL is built.

On a hosted platform you get a sensible default of all of these and very little ability to go beyond it. Sensible defaults are enough when you're competing with nobody. They stop being enough the moment a competitor is doing this properly.

## 2. Mobile is slow and you can't fix it

You run your site through PageSpeed Insights. It tells you the images are oversized and there's too much JavaScript blocking the render. You read the recommendations. Then you realise you have no mechanism to act on any of them.

This is the most frustrating version of the ceiling, because the diagnosis is completely available to you and the treatment isn't. You can't change how images are served, you can't defer the scripts the platform loads, you can't strip the CSS you aren't using.

Page speed is not a vanity metric. It correlates directly with how many people stay long enough to do anything.

## 3. You're fighting the template

Every new page becomes a negotiation. You wanted a three-column comparison table with a highlighted middle option. You got three stacked blocks and a shrug. So you nest some code blocks, add custom CSS through the injection panel, and end up with something that works until the platform updates and it quietly doesn't.

The tell here is your own behaviour: you've started designing around what the builder can do rather than what the page needs to communicate. That's a small tax on every page, forever, and it compounds.

## 4. There's one thing it just won't do

There is always one. A members area. A booking flow that writes into your CRM. Conditional form logic — show these questions only if they picked that option. A properly filterable product catalogue. Multi-currency. A calculator with real rules behind it.

You go looking for a workaround, find a third-party embed, and now you have a feature that lives outside your site, looks slightly wrong, and breaks independently of everything else.

## 5. The bill keeps climbing

The platform subscription is the smallest line. Then there's the scheduling tool, the email tool, the popup tool, the review widget, the forms upgrade. Each is modest on its own. Together they're often more than hosting plus maintenance on a site you'd fully own.

Worth saying plainly: cost alone is a weak reason to migrate. If your Squarespace site is doing everything you need it to, the subscription is money well spent. Cost only matters when you're paying it *and* running into the other four.

## What a migration actually involves

The most common misconception is that migrating means copying the text over. That part is trivial. What matters is everything around it.

**Keeping the URLs Google already knows.** Your existing pages have accumulated authority over years. Any URL that changes needs a 301 redirect to its new equivalent, mapped one by one. Skip this and you don't just lose rankings — you lose them without any obvious cause, three weeks later, when everyone has stopped watching.

**Moving images properly.** Squarespace serves resized versions of your uploads. Pulling those down naively gets you either the original enormous files or the display-size crops. Both are wrong. Images need to come across at correct dimensions and be re-served in modern formats.

**Rebuilding rather than replicating.** A migration is the natural moment to fix the structure, not just port it. The page nobody visits doesn't need to survive. The service buried three clicks deep probably deserves the homepage.

**Building something you can still edit.** The single biggest failure mode of a WordPress migration is ending up with a site that's more powerful and less usable — where every copy change requires a developer. That is a worse outcome than staying on Squarespace. Done right, you should be able to edit your own pages on a Tuesday afternoon without calling anyone.

**Preserving forms and integrations.** Every form, every automation, every analytics event. Tested on the live domain, not staging.

Done well, nobody outside your business notices anything happened, except that the site got faster and you stopped complaining about it.

## Should you actually move?

Honest answer: if only one of the five above applies to you, probably not yet. Migration is real work and real money, and a platform you're 80% happy with is not an emergency.

If two or more apply — particularly the SEO and the "one thing it won't do" — the cost of staying is already higher than the cost of moving. You're just paying it in workarounds instead of invoices.
