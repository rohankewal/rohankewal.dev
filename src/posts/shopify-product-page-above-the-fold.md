Most Shopify product pages I open are doing three jobs at once. Selling the product, explaining the brand, and pushing four other products. All above the fold, all before the visitor has scrolled.

Above the fold has one job. Make it obvious what this is, what it costs, and how to buy it.

Everything else earns its place further down. Not deleted, because most of it is genuinely useful. Just sequenced properly. Here's what I move, and why.

## Reviews: keep the rating, move the wall

The star rating belongs at the top, right under the product title. It's one line, it's instant proof, and it costs you almost no space.

The scrolling wall of twelve reviews does not belong at the top. Nobody reads reviews before they've decided they're interested. Reviews are what people check once they're already considering it and looking for a reason to feel safe about it.

Put too high, that wall pushes the price and the button down the page to serve a need the visitor hasn't developed yet.

Rating up top. Reviews below the description, where they answer the doubts the description just created.

## Upsells and "complete the look": after intent, not before

Cross-sells work on the back of a decision that's already been made. Someone who's decided they want the jacket is genuinely open to the matching scarf. Someone still deciding about the jacket is being handed an alternative to think about instead, which is a reason to leave the page, not a reason to spend more.

Put them below the add-to-cart, or in the cart drawer where the intent is obvious.

Worth remembering: the revenue you attribute to that widget isn't the revenue it generated. It's the revenue minus whatever its position cost you higher up the page.

## Brand story: on the page, not in the middle of the transaction

Your founding story, your sourcing, your ethics. These do real work, especially for anything people think hard about before buying. They belong on the product page.

What they can't do is sit between the price and the button. Every element in that gap is friction on the one action the page exists to produce.

## Subscriptions: pick a default, don't offer a fork

If you sell one-time and subscription, the usual pattern is two equal radio buttons at the top of the page. That's a decision handed to someone who hasn't made the first decision yet, which is whether they want the product at all.

Pick a default. Usually subscription, since it's the better outcome for you and often for them too. Show the saving in plain numbers, like "$32, save 15%". Make switching to one-time obvious and easy.

And put the cancellation terms right there. The unspoken objection to every subscription is "how hard is it to get out of this," and answering it costs you one line.

One decision at a time. Product first, frequency second.

## The thing most stores miss completely

Eleven color options with no guidance isn't choice. It's homework.

If your variants differ in any way that matters, whether that's fit, weight, use case, skin tone, or room size, say so. One line under the swatches ("runs slim, size up if you're between sizes") does more for conversion than most redesigns.

If they only differ visually, then show them properly. Real photography per variant, not a color chip and hope.

Variant paralysis is one of the most common conversion problems in ecommerce and one of the most invisible, because it doesn't look like a bounce. It looks like a slightly longer session and no purchase.

## Two more worth checking while you're in there

**Do your collection pages filter the way people actually shop?** Most stores inherit whatever filters the theme shipped with, which is usually price and size. If your customers shop by occasion, by room, by skin type, or by what it's compatible with, the filters should say so. That's a product and collection setup problem rather than a design problem, and it's usually a couple of hours of work.

**Does your reporting tell you which pages lose the sale?** Nearly every store has this data and never looks at it. A basic funnel per product (sessions, add to carts, checkouts, purchases) tells you straight away whether your problem is traffic, the product page, or checkout. Those are three completely different projects, and most people are guessing which one they're in.

## The order I'd build it in

Starting from scratch, this is the sequence:

1. Product title and star rating
2. Photography, one clear set, not eleven near-identical shots
3. Price, with the subscription default presented clearly
4. Variants, with guidance if they differ in a way that matters
5. Add to cart. One button, unambiguous, always visible on mobile
6. Three to five lines on what this is and who it's for
7. Full description and specs
8. Reviews
9. Shipping, returns, and the questions people email you about
10. Cross-sells and related products

Nothing's been removed. Same page, same content. It's just stopped asking the visitor to hold three questions in their head at once.
